### en-short | Night trains return to European rail timetables
After years of cuts, overnight trains are back on several European routes. Operators say travellers who want to avoid short flights are filling sleeper cabins faster than expected, especially on lines between Germany, Austria and Italy.
The Austrian railway ÖBB runs the largest network under its Nightjet brand and ordered new trains with private sleeping pods. Smaller companies such as European Sleeper have started routes between Brussels, Amsterdam and Prague using refurbished carriages.
The business remains difficult. Track access charges are high, rolling stock is expensive and a train that runs only once a night earns far less per carriage than a day train. Several routes only survive with public support.
Supporters argue the climate benefit justifies that support: a night train journey emits a fraction of the carbon of the same trip by air.
### en-medium | How home batteries are changing the way households use solar power
For most of the past decade, households with solar panels sent their surplus electricity back to the grid during the day and bought power back in the evening. In many countries that arrangement is now changing, and home batteries are moving from a niche product to a mainstream purchase.
The main driver is money. Feed-in tariffs, the price a household receives for exported electricity, have fallen sharply in Germany, the United Kingdom and parts of Australia. In the Netherlands the net-metering scheme that let households offset exports against imports one for one is being phased out from 2027. When exported power earns only a few cents, storing it for use after sunset becomes far more attractive.
Battery prices have also dropped. Lithium iron phosphate cells, which are heavier than the chemistry used in phones but last longer and are less prone to overheating, now dominate home storage. A typical system of ten kilowatt-hours costs roughly half of what a comparable unit cost five years ago, according to installers.
Sizing remains the most common mistake. A battery that is too small fills up before noon on sunny days and contributes little; one that is too large never fully charges in winter, when solar output is lowest. Installers usually recommend matching the battery to the household's evening consumption rather than to the size of the solar array.
Software matters as much as hardware. Modern systems can follow dynamic electricity prices, charging from the grid when power is cheap at night and discharging during the expensive early-evening peak. Some households now earn more from this price arbitrage than from storing their own solar power.
Grid operators are watching closely. Millions of batteries that respond to the same price signals could all charge at the same moment and strain local cables. Several network companies are testing schemes that pay households to let the operator control charging during busy periods.
There are environmental questions as well. Mining lithium and other materials has local costs, and recycling capacity for large batteries is still being built. Manufacturers point out that lithium iron phosphate avoids cobalt and nickel, two of the most problematic materials.
For now, the decision comes down to local prices and habits. A household that uses most of its electricity in the evening and lives in a country with low feed-in tariffs is likely to recover the cost of a battery within eight to twelve years. A household that is mostly home during the day may gain very little.
### en-long | The slow comeback of the city tram
In the middle of the twentieth century, most cities in North America and much of Western Europe tore up their tram networks. Buses were cheaper to buy, did not need tracks and could be rerouted at will, and cars were seen as the transport of the future. Paris removed its last tram line in 1957, and London had already closed its network in 1952.
Seventy years later, the tram is back. France alone has opened more than twenty new networks since Nantes reintroduced trams in 1985. Spain, Morocco, Israel and Australia have followed, and even cities that never fully abandoned trams, such as Amsterdam and Vienna, keep extending their lines. The reasons for the revival say a lot about how cities now think about streets.
The first reason is capacity. A modern tram can carry around three hundred people, about three times as many as a standard bus, with a single driver. On busy corridors that difference reduces operating costs and makes frequent service affordable. Planners often describe the tram as a way to move as many people as a metro line at a fraction of the construction cost, as long as the tram has its own lane.
That condition is crucial. A tram stuck in traffic is no faster than a bus. The cities that report the biggest gains in ridership are those that gave trams segregated tracks and priority at traffic lights. In Bordeaux, where the network opened in 2003, the tram runs on grass-covered tracks through much of the city and travel times are predictable throughout the day.
The second reason is urban renewal. Tram projects are rarely only transport projects. When a line is built, the street is usually rebuilt from facade to facade, with wider pavements, new trees, cycle paths and fewer parking spaces. Bordeaux used its tram project to clear cars from the historic quays along the Garonne, which are now among the most visited public spaces in the city. Studies in several French cities have found that property values near new tram stops rose faster than elsewhere.
Critics say that this effect is exactly the problem. Rising rents near tram lines can push out the residents the line was meant to serve. Some cities now combine tram projects with rules that reserve a share of new housing near stations for lower incomes.
The third reason is perception. Transport researchers have documented what they call a rail bonus: people are more willing to use a tram than a bus on the same route with the same frequency. Explanations vary. Rails show where the service goes and promise that it will still be there next year; trams ride more smoothly; and stops tend to be better designed. Whatever the cause, the effect influences ridership forecasts and therefore investment decisions.
Costs remain the main objection. Building a tram line in a European city typically costs between twenty and forty million euros per kilometre, depending on how many underground utilities must be moved. That is far cheaper than a metro but far more expensive than a bus lane. Bus rapid transit, with dedicated lanes, high-capacity vehicles and stations where passengers pay before boarding, can deliver much of the speed of a tram for a third of the price. Cities such as Bogotá and Istanbul have built large bus networks of this kind.
Supporters of trams answer that the comparison is unfair because buses wear out faster, carry fewer people per driver and rarely trigger the same investment along their routes. Battery-electric buses have narrowed some of these differences. They are quiet and emit no exhaust, which removes one of the strongest arguments trams used to have.
Technology is also changing trams themselves. Several cities have installed sections without overhead wires, using batteries or a power rail that is only live when a tram passes over it. Bordeaux pioneered the ground-level power supply to protect the views of its historic centre, and Nice, Rio de Janeiro and Dubai have since used similar systems. Wireless sections are more expensive and more complex to maintain, so most networks use them only where wires would be unacceptable.
Construction is the most painful phase for residents and shopkeepers. Streets can be closed for two years or more, and businesses along the route often lose customers during the works. Some cities now pay compensation to affected shops, and contractors increasingly build in short sections to limit disruption.
Not every project succeeds. In the United States, several modern streetcar lines have struggled with low ridership because they run in mixed traffic, with low frequencies, on short routes that connect few destinations. Researchers who studied these lines concluded that the vehicle matters far less than the route, the frequency and the priority given at junctions.
Looking ahead, many cities are planning extensions rather than new networks. Lines are being pushed into suburbs and connected to regional rail, sometimes using tram-trains that can run on both city streets and mainline tracks. Karlsruhe in Germany developed this model in the 1990s, and it has since been copied in Kassel, Mulhouse and Sheffield.
The tram's comeback is therefore less a story about nostalgia than about priorities. Where cities are willing to give space to public transport and to rebuild streets around people rather than cars, trams have proved a durable and popular choice. Where they are squeezed into traffic, they disappoint, just as buses do.
